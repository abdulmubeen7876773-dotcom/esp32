ADC Mission 08

Read a Dial with the ESP32 ADC

📖The Story

So far, most of your inputs have been digital. A button is pressed or released. A GPIO is HIGH or LOW. That kind of thinking is powerful, but the real world is rarely only two choices.

Room light changes gradually. A volume knob turns smoothly. Soil becomes wetter or drier by degrees. A battery voltage falls slowly. Mission 08 teaches the ESP32 to measure those in-between values.

🧒Explain Like I'm 12

A digital input is like asking, 'Is the door open?' The answer is yes or no.

An analog input is like asking, 'How open is the door?' It might be closed, halfway open, almost open, or anywhere between.

The ESP32 cannot directly think in smooth voltages, so it uses an ADC. The ADC looks at the voltage and turns it into a number. Low voltage becomes a small number. Higher voltage becomes a bigger number.

ConceptAnalog Values Are In-Between Values

An analog input measures a changing voltage rather than only HIGH or LOW. A potentiometer produces that voltage at its wiper.

With the sketch configured for 12 bits, analogRead() returns raw counts from 0 to 4095. Counts are not volts and are not a calibrated knob-position percentage. analogReadMilliVolts() performs another conversion and returns a calibrated voltage estimate in millivolts.

Engineering Explanation

This example assumes a classic ESP32-WROOM DevKit, GPIO34 (ADC1), and Arduino-ESP32 3.3.2 as the review baseline, not a claim about the latest release. GPIO34 is input-only and has no internal pull-up or pull-down. Other ESP32 families have different ADC pins and ranges.

The potentiometer is a voltage divider between 3V3 and GND. Its wiper can approach the full supply voltage. Never apply 5 V or a negative voltage to GPIO34. Keep external signals within the board's 3.3 V supply rails; attenuation is a measurement setting, not overvoltage protection.

analogReadResolution(12) selects 0–4095 raw counts. analogSetPinAttenuation(POT_PIN, ADC_11db) selects the wider ADC input range. Espressif documents approximately 150–3100 mV for this setting on classic ESP32. A 3.3 V-powered dial can therefore saturate near its upper end; readings near either endpoint are less useful for accurate voltage measurement. A count of 4095 does not prove the voltage is 3.3 V.

analogReadMilliVolts() uses ADC calibration to estimate voltage. It and analogRead() take separate samples, so the displayed pair can differ slightly as the dial moves. Calibration reduces chip-to-chip error; it does not turn the ADC into a precision voltmeter. Check important measurements against known voltages and a suitable meter within the documented measurement range.

On classic ESP32, ADC2 shares hardware with Wi-Fi. Use an ADC1 pin such as GPIO34 for readings while Wi-Fi operates. This restriction is not a universal rule for every ESP32 family. Short wires, stable power and averaging can reduce noise, but cannot correct saturation or unsafe input voltage.

Real WorldReal-World Analogy

A light switch is digital: it is either off or on. A volume knob is analog: it can be quiet, medium, loud, or anywhere between. That is the first analog idea: not two answers, but a range of answers.

A thermometer is another example. Temperature does not jump only between cold and hot. It moves gradually. Analog inputs let a microcontroller notice those gradual changes.

Think of the ADC like a ruler for voltage. The voltage is the real-world length. The ADC number is the ruler marking closest to where the voltage lands. The ruler is useful, but it still has marks, so the ESP32 reports a nearby number rather than an infinite perfect value.

⌨️Arduino Code

Copy this into Arduino IDE, then click Upload.

analog_potentiometer_read.ino 
#include <Arduino.h>

const int POT_PIN = 34;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  analogReadResolution(12);
  analogSetPinAttenuation(POT_PIN, ADC_11db);
  delay(500);
  Serial.println("Mission 08: raw ADC counts and calibrated millivolts");
}

void loop() {
  const uint16_t raw = analogRead(POT_PIN);
  const uint32_t millivolts = analogReadMilliVolts(POT_PIN);
  Serial.print("Raw ADC: ");
  Serial.print(raw);
  Serial.print("  |  Calibrated estimate: ");
  Serial.print(millivolts);
  Serial.println(" mV");
  delay(200);
}

✨ What you'll see

At 115200 baud, the sketch prints its startup message, then lines in this format:

Classic ESP32-WROOM DevKit / ESP32 Dev Module, Arduino-ESP32 3.3.2 review baseline. GPIO34 is ADC1 and input-only. No additional library. Documentation reviewed; compilation and hardware testing were not performed.

Raw counts and millivolts come from separate conversions. ADC_11db does not make the pin 5 V tolerant. Expect saturation near the ends of a 3.3 V-powered potentiometer.

CodeLine-by-line Explanation

  • GPIO34 reads the potentiometer wiper on classic ESP32 ADC1.
  • analogReadResolution(12) explicitly selects 12-bit raw readings.
  • analogSetPinAttenuation() sets the measurement range for this pin, not its electrical safety limit.
  • analogRead() returns uncalibrated counts; analogReadMilliVolts() takes a separate sample and returns a calibrated millivolt estimate.
  • Serial Monitor must use 115200 baud. delay(200) keeps the display readable.

OutputExpected Behaviour

At 115200 baud, the sketch prints its startup message, then lines in this format:

Raw ADC: <count> | Calibrated estimate: <millivolts> mV

The angle-bracket fields stand for readings, not measured results supplied by this tutorial. Turning the dial should change both values through much of its travel. Small fluctuations are expected. Near the ends, readings can flatten or saturate; do not expect a linear 0–3.3 V scale or an accurate knob-position percentage.

MistakesCommon Mistakes

  • Connecting the potentiometer to 5 V

    Many Arduino examples use 5 V boards, but ESP32 GPIO pins are 3.3 V logic.

  • Using a non-ADC pin

    Not every GPIO can read analog voltage.

  • Reading the wrong potentiometer leg

    The outside legs are fixed ends; the middle leg is the changing wiper.

  • Expecting perfectly stable numbers

    ADC readings naturally include small noise and variation.

  • Thinking 4095 means 4095 volts

    The ADC value is a count, not a voltage unit.

  • Thinking analog means exact

    Beginners often expect a slowly turned dial to produce perfectly smooth numbers.

  • Assuming 2048 is always exactly half of 3.3 V

    ADC readings are close estimates, not laboratory-grade measurements.

  • Using ADC2 pins while planning WiFi

    On classic ESP32, ADC2 shares hardware with Wi-Fi.

  • Forgetting the outside potentiometer legs define direction

    Swapping 3.3 V and GND reverses whether clockwise increases or decreases.

  • Treating every analog sensor like a potentiometer

    Potentiometers, LDRs, soil moisture sensors, microphones, and temperature sensors produce different ranges and behavior.

!Troubleshooting

Most ESP32 problems are wiring, power, library, or timing issues. Check these first.

  • The value stays at 0

    Likely cause: GPIO34 may be connected to GND, or the middle pin may not be wired correctly.

    Fix: Trace the potentiometer middle pin to GPIO34 and confirm one outer leg goes to 3.3 V.

  • The value stays near 4095

    Likely cause: The ADC may be saturated near the high end of the dial, or the wiper wire may be on an outer leg.

    Fix: Turn toward GND. If the reading never falls, unplug USB and trace the wiper to GPIO34. Attenuation cannot protect against excessive input voltage.

  • Values change backwards

    Likely cause: The 3.3 V and GND outer legs are swapped.

    Fix: This is safe if both are still 3.3 V and GND. Swap the outer wires if you want clockwise to increase.

  • Values jump around a lot

    Likely cause: Loose breadboard wires, a damaged potentiometer, or noisy power can cause unstable readings.

    Fix: Press wires firmly, try a different potentiometer, and keep jumper wires short.

  • Serial Monitor prints nothing

    Likely cause: The board may not be connected, upload may have failed, or the baud rate may be wrong.

    Fix: Upload again, choose the correct port, and set Serial Monitor to 115200 baud.

TipEngineer Tip

1. Always print the raw ADC value before turning it into a decision. 2. Calibrate with the real circuit, not guessed values. 3. Keep analog wires short when possible. 4. Expect small noise and design around it. 5. Choose ADC1 pins when WiFi may be used later.

?FAQs

  • What is an analog signal?

    An analog signal can change smoothly through many values instead of being only ON or OFF.

  • What is the difference between digital and analog input?

    A digital input gives two answers, HIGH or LOW. An analog input gives a number that represents a voltage level.

  • What does ADC mean?

    ADC means Analog-to-Digital Converter. It turns a voltage into a number the ESP32 can use.

  • Why does ESP32 analogRead() return 0 to 4095?

    The ESP32 ADC commonly uses 12-bit readings. Twelve bits can represent 4096 possible values, numbered 0 through 4095.

  • What voltage should I use with ESP32 analog inputs?

    Use only signals within GND and the board's 3.3 V supply rails. Never use 5 V or negative voltage. On classic ESP32, ADC_11db has an approximate 150–3100 mV measurement range; electrical safety does not guarantee accurate measurement across the full supply range.

  • What is a potentiometer?

    A potentiometer is a variable resistor with a rotating shaft. It can act like a dial that outputs a changing voltage.

  • Why does turning the potentiometer change the number smoothly?

    The middle pin of the potentiometer slides between 0 V and 3.3 V, so the ADC sees many voltage levels between the two ends.

  • Why does my analog value wiggle when I am not touching the dial?

    Small electrical noise, loose wiring, USB power noise, and ADC variation can make readings move by a few counts.

  • Is ADC noise a broken sensor?

    Usually no. Small changes are normal. Real products often average or filter analog readings.

  • Can I use any ESP32 GPIO for analog input?

    No. Only ADC-capable pins can read analog values. For beginner lessons, use a known ADC1 pin such as GPIO34.

  • Why use GPIO34 in this mission?

    On classic ESP32, GPIO34 is an input-only ADC1 pin with no internal pulls. ADC1 avoids the classic ADC2/Wi-Fi conflict. This pin choice does not apply automatically to other ESP32 families.

  • Can GPIO34 control an LED?

    No. GPIO34 is input-only. Use it for reading, not output.

  • What happens if I connect the potentiometer backwards?

    The numbers will move in the opposite direction. Low becomes high and high becomes low, but the ESP32 should still read safely if the ends are 3.3 V and GND.

  • Can analog input read negative voltage?

    No. ESP32 GPIO pins should not receive negative voltage. Keep the signal between GND and 3.3 V.

  • Why do real products use analog inputs?

    Many real-world conditions change gradually, such as light, pressure, position, moisture, battery voltage, and sound level.
