The resistor limits current

An ordinary indicator LED is not a fixed resistance. Its current changes sharply with voltage, so a direct GPIO connection can damage the LED or output. Put a current-limiting resistor in the same series path. A button pull-up resistor serves another purpose; do not substitute its wiring arrangement.

This lesson concerns one low-current indicator, not LED strips, high-power emitters or motor drivers. Some LED modules already include a resistor or driver. Check the exact circuit before adding a resistor or assuming a module can connect directly.

Write down the assumptions

You need the drive voltage available under load, the LED forward voltage at the intended current and a modest target current supported by both LED and board. GPIO HIGH is nominally a logic level, not an ideal bench supply. Loaded output voltage and total chip/board current budgets matter. Absolute-maximum ratings are not recommended operating currents.

Use the actual LED datasheet. Do not assign one forward voltage to every red, green or blue LED. The contributed Blink photos show a green LED but do not establish its exact part, forward voltage or current. A calculation is not a measurement of that photograph.

Work a labelled example

Illustrative assumptions only: drive voltage 3.3 V, LED forward voltage 2.0 V at target current 0.002 A (2 mA). The remaining voltage is 1.3 V. R = (Vdrive − Vf) / I gives 650 ohms. The next larger available value, 680 ohms, gives approximately 1.3 / 680 = 0.00191 A, or 1.91 mA under those assumptions.

This is not a universal 680-ohm recommendation. If the documented LED forward voltage is close to the actual GPIO HIGH voltage, there may be insufficient headroom for a predictable visible result. Do not remove the resistor to compensate. Use a compatible low-current indicator or a suitable driver design.

Check tolerance and power

To estimate maximum current, consider the highest plausible drive voltage, lowest plausible forward voltage and lowest resistance permitted by tolerance. For the same illustrative 3.3 V and 2.0 V, a 680-ohm resistor at minus 5% is 646 ohms; current rises to approximately 2.01 mA. That shows only resistor tolerance, not a complete worst-case check of an unidentified LED.

Resistor dissipation is I²R, or voltage across the resistor multiplied by current. At the nominal 680-ohm example, it is about 0.00249 W. Choose a rating above actual worst-case dissipation with suitable margin. A higher power rating cannot repair a resistance that permits excessive current.

Apply it to the documented Blink circuit

Disconnect USB before rewiring. Follow the linked guide’s logical path: GPIO2 → series resistor → LED anode → LED cathode → GND on the stated classic ESP32 board. A resistor can sit on either side electrically if it remains in that single series path; breadboard rows still determine the physical connections.

Use the Blink guide’s documented parts and the real LED specification. Its 220–330-ohm example and this 680-ohm calculation have different stated assumptions; this lesson does not silently change that guide’s parts list. GPIO2 is a boot-strapping pin on classic ESP32, so retain its upload precautions. Do not connect a GPIO directly to an external supply.

If the LED is dim or off

Unplug power and check polarity, GPIO labels, resistor markings and breadboard connectivity. Verify a suspect resistor with a meter on an unpowered circuit. A lit board power indicator does not prove the external LED path is correct.

For a dim LED, inspect its specification and calculation before reducing resistance. If something becomes hot, unplug and investigate; do not increase GPIO drive strength as a shortcut. Measure current only with a correctly configured meter in series and suitable supervision. A current-range meter placed across a supply can short it.

Try the calculation before the hardware

Choose two hypothetical, documented LED forward voltages and calculate current for the same resistor. Explain which case produces more current. Record assumed drive voltage, Vf, target current, chosen standard value and power estimate.

Then use the complete Blink guide for wiring and code. This calculation lesson intentionally has no new sketch: software does not replace the missing electrical specification.
